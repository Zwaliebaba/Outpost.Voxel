## 1. Summary

Outpost is a real-time strategy game in space, in the mold of Warzone 2100. The player commands a station and the ships it builds, from a strategic camera, in a sector shared with one opponent and a neutral trader. What sets it apart is a combination: the player sculpts the hulls of ships and station structures voxel by voxel, and fields them by the dozen in Warzone's kind of match, where a hull's shape decides how it flies and fights. Players already build what they fight with in From the Depths, Cosmoteer and Avorion; none of those puts such hulls into a symmetric match of station, research and economy (panel M1). Outside the match, the player sculpts hulls, places the mounts that will hold their modules, and keeps the results in a library of designs, each with its named variants. In the match, the player mines ore from asteroids and wrecks, grows the station structure by structure, claims fields with outposts, researches, fields designs from the library, refits them to other variants as the match demands, and fights. Research unlocks modules and size classes, and toughens armor and upgrades modules on every ship at once, as Warzone's does; traders sell modules not yet researched, at a premium. Damage is per voxel: a shot removes the voxels it hits, a module fails as its voxels go, a ship whose command module fails is lost and left as a wreck to salvage, and one whose reactor fails detonates.

The engine keeps what `SpaceScene.md` built: the authoritative server on a thread of its own, the transport, the snapshots, the placements and the splat. The game changes it in four places (§11). Designs travel as data, not as files both sides already hold. Every message is encoded for one side, so that fog of war holds for shots, damage and mining as well as for what a snapshot shows, and a shot tells a side where it is, never where it came from. The renderer's record buffer grows during a match, and its placements mask the voxels shot away. And the levers of SpaceScene §7.6 must survive damage, because in a battle every ship is damaged. The load also moves: a battle's voxels stay within what the space scene draws today, but its placements grow about nineteenfold (§11.2).

What decides a fight is written down before combat is built. A design's figures come from its geometry, as one profile that flight, the HUD and the opponent all read (G29). A weapon aims at its target's silhouette, a shell leads its target, a ship keeps its target, a squadron bears in the formation its designs ask for, and a tick's shots resolve at once, the same for every side (G33, G57, G58, G62, G71, G72), by one contract for what a shot meets (G39); and a side's fleet is held to a budget of command points (G56). Each milestone then ends at a gate: a measured figure against a threshold, with the change of course it triggers (G28), measured against the budgets of G32 on samples sized to decide it (G73). The battle milestone's gate is the shape test, which asks whether designs of different shapes and sizes win in different situations, and it runs again whenever prices or rules change.

## 2. Scope

**In scope of this concept:** the player and the match (§3); the loop (§4); construction (§5); stations (§6); the economy, research and trade (§7); combat and damage (§8); command and control (§9); the opponent (§10); what the engine must change (§11); and the next phase's slice, milestones and gates (§12).

**The slice** is what the next phase builds: a skirmish in one seeded, symmetric sector, the player against one opponent, with the whole loop in it at a small size: a research tree of about twenty items, with at most three labs a side; a catalogue of about a dozen modules; two weapons; one resource; and at most two outposts a side. It is started from the command line, as the space scene is today, and it ships the two-library runner as a tool (G69).

**Out of scope, and why:**

- **Multiplayer between machines** (G8). The UDP transport, its reliable channel and `Server.exe` wait for a skirmish worth playing; SpaceScene §6.1 already says what they add. Delta encoding of snapshots comes with them. The first mode after the slice is the library challenge, which needs none of them (G69).
- **A campaign,** and the mission scripting it needs.
- **Several resources, and ores that gate materials** (G10). Ore has grades, which set what it is worth and never what it builds (G52), and salvage is in: a wreck's voxel pays half the price of the hull voxel it was (G63).
- **Shields,** which G3 names among the modules. They change what a shot does before it reaches a voxel, which combat designs once per-voxel damage is measured (§8.4).
- **Damage types.** Counters come from geometry (G60); a table of damage by weapon and material class is held in reserve as gate 5's change of course.
- **Tracing a shot to its shooter,** which a sensor module can do after the slice (G54), and **detonations that damage their neighbors,** held in reserve in case tight formations win (G51).
- **Fighters, carriers, boarding, crews, diplomacy, more than two sides, and several sectors.** `Universe` stays free for the last (ADR-017).
- **Designs the opponent invents.** It fields an authored library, or any library the command line names (§10); a generator is a design of its own.
- **Audio.** The game needs it and `NeuronClient` has none; it is a design and an ADR of its own.
- **Menus, and saving and loading a match.** A match replays from its command log (G41), which is not a save.
- **A release.** Outpost is the owner's project, not a product (G70), so the exclusions above stay exclusions, not a roadmap.

## 3. The player and the match

**The player is a commander (G1).** They never fly a ship. They select ships and structures and give orders, from a camera over the sector's plane (§9); the orbit and chase cameras of SpaceScene §13 stay, for looking at one ship.

**A skirmish (G8)** is one sector built from a seed, as `Sector` is today (ADR-017), and symmetric under a half turn about its center, so that neither start is favored (G66): two starting positions far apart, asteroid fields near each and richer ones between them, and a lone trader station at the center, or two that the half turn maps onto each other. Each side starts with a station core, two constructor ships, a small escort, a stated bank of credits, and no research beyond tier zero (§7.2). The bank, and whether the core alone powers the station at the start, are G-M4's to set, and G-M4 measures how soon a side earns its first credit (§12.2). The skirmish's options may handicap either side with a bank and an income multiplier, shown to both (G67). The world runs at 30 ticks a second, as now.

**Victory.** A side loses when its station core is destroyed (G20). The core is the skirmish's own design, the same for both sides (G48). It can be defended and surrounded by structures, and constructors repair it, except its command module, whose damage only accumulates; it cannot be rebuilt. Once every field is dry, or at minute 60 if that comes first, each core's command module wears down at a fixed rate (G36), so every match has an end: the more damaged core fails first, and cores that fail on the same tick draw. The clock counts ticks, as R21 asks, so a side that leaves a field unmined cannot hold the wear off.

**Scale (G11, G56).** Dozens of ships a side, up to about fifty, and perhaps fifteen structures, outposts included. The server holds each side to a budget of command points: a ship costs its size class's points, miners and constructors included, so an economy's ships compete with a fleet's for the budget, and constructors have a cap of their own, which G-M4 sets. The budget and the costs are catalogue data, set so that a side at its budget stays within §11.2's counts. Ships are the size of the two ship assets and between them: from the frigate's 1,181 voxels to the capital ship's 10,747 (SpaceScene §4), each within its class's voxel budget (G40). If the battle's gate finds per-voxel damage too dear, the budget gives way first, toward thirty ships a side, after the lifetimes and the levers and before any coarser damage cell (G44). A match is meant to last twenty to forty minutes. That is a target for tuning the economy and the tree, not a figure anyone has measured, and G-M5's batch measures it (§12.2).

**Movement (G7).** Ships move on the sector's main plane, where the stations already lie in a flattened disc (ADR-017), and change between three altitude bands: the plane, one above it and one below. The bands have a job, because a side's own hulls block its shots (G39): a ship a band up fires over its own front rank, and a band decides which of a design's dorsal or ventral mounts bear (§5.4). The bands are spaced to clear the largest banked hull and kept closer than the shortest weapon's range. Orders are given on the plane, a group's band is a standing setting, and a grid is drawn under the selection (§9).

## 4. The loop

```
 asteroid fields ──┐
                   ├─mine──► refinery ──► credits ──┬──► structures: the station and its outposts grow (§6)
 wrecks ───────────┘                                ├──► research: modules, armor, size classes (§7.2)
    ▲                                               ├──► production and refit: library designs, as variants (§5.6)
    │                                               └──► traders: modules not yet researched (§7.3)
    │
    └── fleets fight over the fields and the trader's ground ──► the enemy's station core (§3)
```

Ore is the one resource (G10): the voxels of asteroids and of wrecks, which pay half the price of the hull they were (G63), and credits are what a refinery makes of it. Every choice spends credits: a structure, an outpost, a research project, a ship, a refit, a module bought. The fields near a start are small, safe and low in grade; the higher grades lie between the starts, and so does the trader, so expansion and fighting happen in the same place: an outpost claims a field there (G65), and the wrecks a fight leaves are salvage for whoever holds the ground. Ore and credits are working names (§14, question 20).

## 5. Construction

### 5.1 Designs, mounts, fits and variants (G3, G5, G12, G35)

A **design** is a hull and its mounts. The hull is voxels, sculpted one at a time, in one or more parts of at most 256³ each (R14, N2). A **mount** is a place for a module: a named hardpoint (NVF §4.1) whose type says what kind of module it holds (`engine`, `weapon`, `mining` and so on) and whose size class says how large. The box a mount's largest module fills holds no hull voxel, and it touches the hull (§5.5). Every design has exactly one mount for a command module.

A **module** is an authored voxel model, an `.nvf` of its own, with a palette of its own (S8): reactor, thruster, weapon, mining laser, cargo hold, refinery, constructor bay, sensor, and the station's roles (§6). What it does, its thrust, its draw on the reactors, its damage, is gameplay data in a catalogue and never in its file (N8).

A **fit** is the module at each mount, and a **variant** is a named fit, of which the library keeps several for each design, the default first (G35). The hull and its mounts are made in the library, outside the match. Variants are kept there too, and the refit screen can make another in the match, from the modules the side has researched or bought (G5). So the slow, creative work of sculpting happens where no clock runs, and the match asks only for Warzone's kind of choice: which variant to build, and which to refit to.

A fitted design draws as one placement for each part of its hull and one for each module at its mount (SpaceScene §7.1). A mount is turned by one of the cube's 24 rotations in its part's space, so a design's placements are cube-symmetric to one another, and a structure, which stands upright and quarter-turned, keeps every placement on the aligned splat (§6).

### 5.2 The library

The library is the player's own collection of designs, kept between matches. Each design is an `.nvf` file for its hull and mounts, with a small file of game data beside it for its name, its material classes (§5.3) and its variants. A design is known by the hash of its content, as a model is known by its file's hash today (SpaceScene §6.2), so a library entry cannot be confused with an edited copy of itself.

Until the in-game designer's sculpting core (G-M6, G45), designs are authored in MagicaVoxel and converted by `NvfImport` (NVF §6): the hull as parts, and the mounts as markers named `<part>@<type>.<name>` (NVF §5), which MagicaVoxel turns by exactly the cube's 24 rotations. That keeps N1, since the game never reads `.vox`, and it means the first designs, the player's and the opponent's alike, exist before any editor does. How a mount's name carries its size class is G-M1's to fix, and from G-M1 a command-line tool prints each design's profile (§5.4), so that a design authored in MagicaVoxel shows its figures. The in-game designer then writes the same files.

### 5.3 Materials and color (G13, G24, G55)

The hull is built from armor of two classes, light and heavy (G3, G13). A class has one density and one price per voxel, and a toughness: the damage a voxel of it takes before it goes. R14 fixes a voxel's record at 32 bits with a four-bit color, and the owner fixed the palette at sixteen entries. This concept keeps both: each of a design's sixteen palette entries pairs a color with a material class (G13), and what the design decides is where its heavy armor goes. The pairing is gameplay data, kept in the design's game data rather than its `.nvf` (N8), so neither the record nor the renderer changes.

Research reaches the hull where it stands, as Warzone's alloys do: a mark of armor raises its class's toughness on every hull of the side at once, fielded ones included, at no cost beyond the research (G55). The server keeps each voxel's damage taken, and a voxel goes when its damage reaches its class's current toughness, so a damaged hull gains from the mark as a whole one does. Since a class's density and price never change, research changes no design's mass, profile or price, and the profiles that gate 2 checks are the ones the match flies. A refit or a repair never changes a hull's armor.

The owner chose the pairing over the alternative, the record's four zero bits, 28 to 31, as a material of its own (G13), and N-M1 built NVF's voxel record as NVF §4 has it ([ADR-019](ADR/ADR-019-nvf-format.md)). If sixteen entries prove too few, those bits remain the way out, but they are now a major version of NVF (NVF §4.6) as well as a change to R14.

**The side's color.** One entry of every design's and every module's palette shows the side's color, and the renderer gives each side its own variant of each palette. Placements already name their palette (ADR-014), so a side's color costs a palette per model and side, and nothing per voxel. It also costs the player one of the sixteen entries (G24).

### 5.4 Performance follows from geometry (G18, G29)

Freedom invites the solid brick and the needle. A design's performance therefore comes from its voxels and its variant, so that shape matters:

- **Mass** is the sum of its voxels', by material class, and its modules'. **Acceleration** is its thrusters' thrust over its mass, and **turning** follows from its moment of inertia, which a long hull has more of.
- **Power.** Reactors supply it and every other module draws it. A variant that draws more than it supplies is refused. When damage leaves a ship short, its modules stop in the reverse of an order the player sets, weapons last by default, and until that order has an interface, all of them slow in proportion (G46); a ship ordered to retreat powers its thrusters first (G59).
- **Arcs.** A weapon fires, a thruster thrusts and a sensor sees only along lines clear of its own hull (G38), so a gun or an engine buried in armor does nothing, and one on a spine works almost everywhere. The line runs along the mount's +Z, the way NVF's hardpoint frame already points (NVF §4.1).
- **Silhouette.** Shots hit real geometry and aim at it (§8.1), so a large hull is a large target, and a hollow one is hit where its voxels are.
- **Cost and time.** A design costs its voxels by material class, plus its modules, and takes a shipyard a time that grows with both.
- **Size classes.** A class is a voxel budget within a box, for ships and structures alike (G40), and it decides the shipyards that can build a design and the command points a ship of it costs (G56); research unlocks the larger classes. A box alone would not do: a solid brick in the frigate's box holds 99.5 % of the capital ship's voxels, by arithmetic from SpaceScene §4.
- **The profile** (G29). The shared game library computes one profile for each design and variant: its mass, center of mass, inertia and thrust per axis; each mount's clear arc, walked with `GridWalk`; for a fixed set of directions, its silhouette, from which weapons draw their aim (G57), and the armor over its command module and each reactor; and its price, build time, size class and command points. Flight takes each ship's limits from it, a design card shows it in the library and in production, refit and inspection, the opponent reads it, and a battle updates it as voxels and modules go.

These are principles. The numbers are tuned in play, and the test of them is whether designs of different shapes and sizes win in different situations, which the battle milestone's gate asks, and asks again as the numbers change (G28, G73, §12.2).

### 5.5 Validation

The server validates a design whenever one enters a match, since it never trusts a client, and the designer validates it as it is saved. A design is refused, by name as the readers refuse (SpaceScene §6.2), when it has no command-module mount or more than one; when its voxels are not one piece, face to face, around that mount; when two of its parts put a voxel in one cell (G37); when a mount's box holds a hull voxel, shares a cell with another mount's box or shares no face with a hull voxel, or its rotation is not one of the cube's 24; when a palette entry names a material class the catalogue lacks; or when it fits no size class's box and voxel budget (G40). The same checks hold between a station's structures, which share one grid on whole positions and quarter turns (S18), so they are exact. A variant is refused when a module does not match its mount's type and size, or when its modules draw more power than its reactors supply. Whether a side may field a valid design, given its research and what it has bought, is a separate question the match asks (§7.2).

### 5.6 Production, refit and repair in the match

A shipyard builds one variant at a time, within its side's command budget (G56), paying for it as it goes, and a design under construction does nothing until it is complete. The ship appears voxel by voxel: server and client derive its build order at load, breadth-first through face neighbors from the command module's mount, ties broken by record index and parts taken in part order (G43), and its placement draws a growing prefix of its records in that order, which a placement's record range already allows (ADR-014). Voxel ids, masks and damage events follow that order, while the file keeps the `.vox`'s (NVF §4.3).

Production and refit orders name a variant (G35). A refit to a variant is an order to a selection: each ship goes to the nearest shipyard of its class, docks (G61), queues, refits and rejoins its group. The refit screen edits a variant once and shows, as it changes, the power margin, mass, acceleration, turn rate, arcs, price and time (§5.4). A refit charges the incoming modules in full. The outgoing ones go into the side's stock, repaired first at repair's price if damaged, and the next ship that needs one takes it from there; a failed one is scrapped, and a module is never turned back into credits (§14, question 35).

A shipyard also repairs a ship docked at it: its lost hull voxels at their class's price, and a damaged module at the module's price times the share of it lost. A failed module is replaced by a refit. A ship fitted with a bought module consumes it when it is built or refitted (§7.3). Neither a refit nor a repair changes a hull's armor, which research toughens where it stands (G55).

## 6. Stations (G9, G17)

A station is a tree of **structures**, each a design like a ship's: a hull with mounts, fitted with the module of its role. A structure lives while its role module works, as a ship lives while its command module does (§8.3). A side's first station grows from its **station core**: the skirmish's own design, the same for both sides, with a fixed number of connectors (G48). It carries the side's command, and its loss ends the match (§3). A side may root up to two more at **outposts** (G65). The roles in the slice:

| Structure | Its role module | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Station core | Command | Holds the side; its connectors start the first station |
| Outpost | Outpost | Roots a station near a field, with power of its own; its loss is not the match's (G65) |
| Refinery | Refinery | Turns the ore delivered to it into credits |
| Shipyard | Shipyard | Builds, refits and repairs designs up to its size class (§5.6) |
| Lab | Lab | Runs one research project at a time (§7.2) |
| Reactor | Reactor | Powers its station |
| Turret | Weapon | Defends: the ships' weapon modules, on a structure |
| Sensor array | Sensor | Sees far (§9) |

**Outposts** (G65). A constructor founds an outpost within reach of an asteroid field, paying as it builds, as it builds any structure. An outpost is the skirmish's own design, smaller than the core and the same for both sides, with connectors and a reactor of its own, so its station starts powered. It is not fatal to lose: a destroyed outpost leaves its station standing, unpowered. A side holds at most two in the slice, so a field between the starts becomes a claim to hold, as Warzone's derricks are, and a refinery there takes the haul out of mining.

**Power.** A ship's reactors power its own modules (§5.4); a station's structures share theirs. A connector joins only the two structures built at it, so each station is a tree, rooted at the core or at an outpost (G46). Every reactor module in a structure connected to its root supplies that station, every structure of it draws from it, and stations share nothing (G26). When draw exceeds supply, structures stop in a set order, turrets last (G46).

**Growth.** A constructor ship builds a structure from the library at a free connector of a station, within its root's build radius (G61), paying as it builds, and the structure appears voxel by voxel as a ship does (§5.6). The radius keeps each station where its root is, so no chain of structures creeps across the sector. Connectors are hardpoints of the structures' hulls, with a type of their own, and a structure's size class bounds it as a ship's does (G40). A structure stands upright, turned by quarter turns about the vertical, as S18 has every station stand, so every structure draws with the aligned splat and every voxel center is exact (SpaceScene §7.2).

**Repair.** Constructors repair structures at a price per voxel and at a rate that G-M4 sets, except the core's command module, whose damage only accumulates (G48).

**Destruction.** Each structure is an entity of its own, standing still (G17): it takes damage and fails on its own, leaving a wreck, or detonates if a reactor of its own fails (§8.3). A destroyed structure takes its connectors with it but not the structures beyond them, which stay where they are, unpowered while no connector joins them to their root.

## 7. Economy, research and trade

### 7.1 Ore and credits (G10, G19, G42, G52, G63)

Ore is the voxels of asteroids, and of wrecks. Mining ships, with a mining laser and a cargo hold in their fit, cut voxels out of an asteroid or a wreck as a shot cuts them out of a hull (§8), and carry the ore to a refinery, which turns it into credits. So the machinery of per-voxel damage serves both, and a field visibly shrinks as it is mined. Fields are finite, which pushes both sides toward the rich ones between them.

**Grades** (G52). Ore is graded by its asteroid's palette entry, as catalogue data. The richer fields between the starts hold the higher grades, so where to cut is a choice. It stays one resource (G10). A refinery module lets a ship serve as a drop-off, so that a far field need not be hauled home from, and that ship is a target worth hunting; an outpost's refinery does the same from a station (G65). Without either, a miner hauling from the middle would earn about 0.39 of what one earns at home, on the first review's model (F2.5).

**Salvage** (G63). A wreck's voxel pays half the price of the hull voxel it was, whatever the grade of the field around it, and a wreck's modules pay nothing. So salvage never repays more than half the hull the other side lost: on the panel's arithmetic, a wreck that paid as ore at half the first review's rate would have repaid a capital ship's hull 2.4 times over (B4).

**The exchange rate** (G42). An ore voxel buys a fixed number of hull voxels, by its grade, counted in light-class voxels, and the number need not be whole. G-M4 fixes the rate first, and commits a budget table as its tuning contract: income per miner, the prices of fleets, stations and outposts, the command budget and its costs by class (G56), the fields' totals and when they run dry, the minute by which a side finishes half the tree (G64), and the salvage share. On the first review's model the rate lies near one ore voxel to five hull voxels, since at parity the stated scale cannot be paid for (F2.2). The fields are sized so that the dry-sector rule rarely fires (G36).

Asteroids come from the sector's seed, as the stars come from the sky's (SpaceScene §11.2): a few authored asteroid models, placed and turned by quarter turns, so that they draw with the aligned splat and cost the snapshots nothing. The generator places half of them, and the other half is the first turned a half turn about the center (G66). What mining removes reaches each side whose sensors see it, as damage does (§8.2, G30). Since both sides must build the same asteroids from the seed, the generator is shared code, and it places them at whole coordinates, which no build rounds differently and which make an asteroid's clearance exact (§9).

### 7.2 Research (G2, G55, G64)

Labs research, one project each at a time, for credits and time, and a side may build at most three. A side starts with tier zero: the first mark of each armor class, and the command module, reactor, thruster, weapon, mining laser, cargo hold and constructor bay its start needs. The tree unlocks, in branches:

- **armor,** marks of toughness for each class of §5.3, which reach every hull of the side at once (G55);
- **modules,** each in marks: a thruster, then a better thruster, which reaches a ship when it is built or refitted;
- **upgrades** to modules already known, which reach every such module the side has fielded at once, as Warzone's research does (G55);
- **size classes,** and the shipyards that build them;
- **industry:** a refinery's yield and a shipyard's speed.

Warzone 2100's tree has 390 topics in multiplayer and 413 in its campaign, as its data files count them (panel review). The slice's has about twenty, sized and priced so that a side with three labs finishes about half of it by the end of a median match (G64): projects take minutes, their prices rise by tier, and no first project should be the right one in most matches, which the batch reads. So the order of research is a choice, and a trader always has a step beyond a side's research to sell (§7.3). A design cannot be fielded until the side has researched its size class, and researched or bought every module of its variant. The library keeps it until it can be fielded.

### 7.3 Traders (G4, G25)

A trader is a neutral station that the sector's seed places on the ground between the starts: one at the center, or two that the half turn maps onto each other (G66). It sells modules one step beyond what the side's research has reached, from a fixed catalogue, at a premium, with a stock that runs down as it sells and refills slowly; it is not a simulated market. A module bought joins the side's stock and is consumed when a ship fitted with it is built or refitted. Buying never teaches the side the module. So buying is the shortcut to a few strong ships early, and research the way to field many. Since a side finishes about half the tree (G64), a trader has something to sell it until the end.

A trader deals with a side only while that side has a ship within its trade radius and the other has none, which makes its ground worth holding. It shows every ship within its radius to each side with a ship there, so that a refused trade gives nothing away through the fog, and it sells each side from a stock of its own (G25). G-M4 states the premium, the stock and the refill. In the slice traders cannot be attacked (G25).

## 8. Combat and damage

### 8.1 Weapons, aim and targets (G33, G47, G57, G58, G60, G62, G71)

The slice has two: a mass driver, whose shells fly at a finite speed and can miss a ship that moves, and a laser, a beam that hits at once, at short range, for as long as it fires. Each is a module at a weapon mount, on a ship or on a turret, and fires within its arc (§5.4). Each has a range, measured in three dimensions from its muzzle to the target's nearest voxel, and an order to attack closes to it through the target's clearance (G47, G61). Where a weapon aims, how a shot leads, which target a ship keeps and how it turns to bear are rules of combat, the same for every side (G33), and they pick the winning hull; the concept chooses them here, and the battle milestone's design sets their numbers.

**Aim** (G57). A weapon aims at a voxel drawn from its target's silhouette as the shooter sees it, from the target's profile (G29), with `PcgHash` of the seed (R21). The mass driver draws a new voxel for every shell, so it erodes; the laser holds its line while it fires, so it drills. A targeting stance or "attack this module" draws from that module's silhouette instead (§9). So no shape is missed for free: a ring or a hollow shell is hit where its voxels are, which fire at the center of mass would have missed through the hole (panel C1).

**Leading and evasion** (G58). A shell is fired at where its target would be if it held its velocity. A ship in combat jinks within its profile's acceleration, on a pattern drawn from `PcgHash` of the seed, so its agility, its thrust over its mass (G18), decides how many shells miss it, and nothing evades the laser.

**Counters** (G60). Counters come from geometry, not from damage types: every weapon damages every material alike. The mass driver is the weapon against large, slow hulls, which cannot jink its shells aside, and the laser the weapon against small, agile ones, which it need not lead. Gate 5 tests weapon against size as well as shape, and a table of damage by weapon and material class is held in reserve as its change of course, if geometry alone leaves one design or weapon dominant (§12.2).

**Targets** (G71). Ships choose targets in range for themselves; the player's orders choose for them (§9). A ship keeps its target until the target is lost, leaves its weapon's range or arc, or an order changes it, since a laser drills only while held on one line. It prefers a target its fire reaches clear of its own side's hulls, and a weapon whose line meets such a hull holds fire; ties are broken with `PcgHash` of the seed.

**Bearing** (G62). Bringing arcs to bear is a ship behavior on the server, the same for every side: a nose-armed design charges, and a broadside design orbits at weapon range with its covered side inward. A squadron flies the formation its designs' bearing asks for, line abreast for designs that fire ahead and line astern for broadside designs, and turns as one, so that its ships' lines of fire toward the target clear one another; the "break and bear" stance lets each ship maneuver alone (§9).

### 8.2 Shots are events (G15, G30, G39, G54, G72)

A shot is a world event, as a detonation is (SpaceScene §5.5). A shell flies in a straight line at a constant velocity, so its flight is a pure function of its position and velocity at any tick and the time, and every side that sees it draws the same shell. A side receives a shell as its position and velocity at the tick it enters that side's sensors, and a beam as the part of it those sensors see; no message tells a side where or when a shot it did not see was fired (G54). A shell's line gives a bearing, never a range, so firing does not give the shooter away, and tracing a shot back to its origin is a sensor's capability, which waits until after the slice.

The server alone decides what a shot hits, and it resolves a tick's shots at once (G72). It sweeps each shell and each beam, by G39's contract, through the grids of the designs and asteroids in its way as they stood when the tick began, with the walk `NeuronCore` already has (`GridWalk`); then it applies the damage summed over every shot; then it searches the connectivity of each damaged entity once. No side's shots are resolved before another's, so a mutual kill is mutual, and a mirrored duel gives the same result with its sides swapped. It sends what it removed as a damage event to each side that sees it: the entity, the tick and the voxels gone. A beam is resolved every tick it fires, and sent as the same events.

**The contract** (G39). A shot meets the enemy's hulls and asteroids. It stops at its own side's hulls without damaging them, so a ship cannot fire through its own front rank but can fire over it from another band (§3), and no side can cut its own hull. It passes wrecks and traders. It spends its damage voxel by voxel along its path, carries the rest on, and applies its reach where it stops, so that a spaced skin of armor stops no more than its voxels are worth. A weapon's shot stops at an asteroid and removes nothing, and a mining laser meets asteroids and wrecks only.

### 8.3 Damage (G6, G14, G51, G59)

Every voxel takes damage up to its class's toughness (§5.3). A hit damages the voxels at and around where it lands, by the weapon's damage and reach. The server keeps each entity's damage taken per voxel, and removes a voxel when its damage reaches its class's toughness, which research may since have raised (G55); a client keeps only which voxels are gone, one bit each: 1,344 bytes for a ship the size of the capital ship, by arithmetic.

- **Modules.** A module's voxels are hit as the hull's are. Its output falls as they go, and below a share of them it fails.
- **Cut off.** After each tick's damage the server finds what is still connected, face to face, to the command module (G72). What is not becomes a **wreck**: an entity of its own, with the voxels it took, drifting on with the velocity it had, as SpaceScene §5.5 has a chunk that breaks off. A ship cut in two is thus a ship and a wreck (G6).
- **Lost.** A ship whose command module fails is lost, and becomes a wreck with the voxels it has left, for miners to salvage (G51). A ship whose reactor module fails detonates instead: it is lost at once, and its remaining voxels become debris, as a detonation's do today, which nobody can salvage. Aiming for a reactor therefore denies the salvage (§9). In the slice a detonation damages nothing near it; damage to its neighbors is held in reserve, in case the batch shows tight formations winning (G51).
- **Warning** (G59). A ship's condition leads with its weakest vital module, its command module or any reactor, against the share at which it fails (§9). A ship ordered to retreat powers its thrusters before its weapons (G46). Damage is tuned so that a duel between ships of equal cost lasts at least three times the time the loser takes to turn half a turn: 12 s for a frigate and 67.5 s for a capital ship at ADR-017's turn rates, by arithmetic. So a retreat can be ordered before the end, and gate 5 measures it.
- **Wrecks** are salvage: a mining laser cuts them as it cuts an asteroid, and each voxel pays half the price of the hull voxel it was (G63). **Debris** is scenery. Ships fly through both, as they fly through debris now. Debris lasts as long as the skirmish's debris lifetime, which starts at about a minute and is tuned on the bench; a wreck lasts until it is mined out or its own, longer lifetime runs out, which G-M4 tunes for salvage and gate 4 holds to the frame (S19, G50).

A structure takes damage the same way, with its role module as its command (§6): it leaves a wreck when that fails, and detonates when a reactor of its own does.

### 8.4 What the numbers must show

Per-voxel damage at fifty ships a side is the concept's largest cost, in three places at once: the server's sweeps and connectivity searches, the damage events on the wire, and the renderer's masks, with the levers of SpaceScene §7.6 held on damaged placements (§11.1). G-M2, the battle milestone, measures all three on a battle against G32's budgets before anything is optimized: the tick at gate 3, with each side at the heaviest fleet its command budget allows, and the aftermath's worst frame at gate 4 (§12.2). If they fail, G44 says what gives way, in order.

## 9. Command and control

**The camera** looks down on the sector's plane: it pans, zooms, turns and tilts, between a battle zoom, where a voxel covers at least a pixel, and a sector zoom, where ships and structures draw as icons over their coarse models (G49), with the minimap beside both. A voxel covers a pixel at about 1,375 units, by arithmetic for a 45° view over 1,080 pixels (SpaceScene §11.1), and fights frame near that by default. Ownership shows through the overlay at every zoom. Each ship stands on a line dropped to the plane, and a grid is drawn under the selection, so its band reads at a glance. The orbit and chase cameras of SpaceScene §13 stay, for looking at one ship.

**Selection** is by click and by box. A click picks the nearest entity whose projected box, grown to at least about 10 pixels, holds the cursor, and never picks debris or a wreck; a box selects the entities whose centers it holds. Inspecting and targeting a module use the exact pick: the visibility buffer's voxel id names the placement (SpaceScene §7.3), the placement names the entity and the module, and a readback of that one pixel delivers it within a couple of frames. Selections go into control groups on the number keys, and the debug views move behind a modifier (SpaceScene §13).

**Orders:** move, on the plane and into a band; attack, which closes through its target's clearance to its weapon's range (G47, G61); attack-move; stop; hold; guard; retreat for repair, as a standing order, which powers thrust first (G59); mine; dock; build a structure, or found an outpost (G65); produce, refit to a variant and repair; research; trade. A targeting stance (engines, weapons, nearest) and "attack this module" choose what a ship aims for, from that module's silhouette (G57), with the command module left off the list, so that no salvage-keeping kill is one click away (G33). A group moves as squadrons of up to about a dozen ships of one speed class, whose rows extend ADR-017's slots in the formation their designs' bearing asks for: line abreast for designs that fire ahead, line astern for broadside designs, turning as one, so that no slot lies in another's line of fire toward the target (G62). The "break and bear" stance lets each ship of a squadron maneuver alone. The skirmish allows orders while it is paused, and the client draws its own marker for an order at once, since the order shows in a snapshot only after the next tick and the interpolation delay (ADR-018).

**Paths.** The plane is open but not empty: structures, traders and asteroids are obstacles, each by its voxels (G61). An obstacle's occupied cells, grown by a ship's radius and a margin, are its clearance. A structure's cells lie on the station's grid of whole positions and quarter turns (S18), and an asteroid's at whole coordinates (§7.1), so a clearance is exact, and a thin structure blocks only where it stands, not the sphere around it that ADR-017's keep-outs drew (panel C5). Ships find paths around the clearances, steer along them with ADR-017's flight model (pure pursuit, bank and slot) within their own profiles' limits (G29), and keep apart from one another by steering, in three dimensions and across bands, never into a clearance, which amends ADR-017 (§13). An order to attack, mine, dock, build or unload closes through its target's clearance to its own range, and only such an order enters one. Ships no longer pass through one another, but they still pass through wrecks and debris.

**Fog of war.** Each side sees what its ships' and structures' sensors reach, and every message it receives holds only that (G21, G30). The server already decides what reaches each client, so a side cannot see past its sensors however its client is changed. So protocol version 2 is encoded per session from G-M1, which ADR-015 forecloses today (§11.1), and fog is switched on in G-M5. Firing does not give the shooter away: a shot reaches a side only once it enters that side's sensors, and only as where it is and how it moves there (G54).

## 10. The opponent (G16)

The opponent plays the same game through the same door: a session over a loopback, as the client has. It receives its side's messages, fog included, and sends commands; it never touches the world's objects, and the server refuses any command that names another side's entity, structure, design or stock (G34). So it cannot cheat by accident, every rule the player meets it meets, and it exercises the protocol as a second client would. It runs in the server's process, stepped with the server once a tick at one fixed point, as the bench steps it (ADR-018), and the server logs every command it applies, with its world tick and its session, so that a skirmish replays from its seed, its build, its libraries and that log (G41). It budgets its thinking in work, never in time, and draws randomness only from `PcgHash` of the seed (G53).

It fields any library the command line names, by default its own, which Claude Code authors unseen by the owner, so that the libraries' author is not also their only opponent. At each size class its own library holds at least three designs with distinct profiles, the seed draws which it fields, and a CI test checks that every one of them validates, fits within power, and can be reached by research or a trader. It researches and trades by the same rules as the player to reach its designs, and fields them within the same command budget (G56). Its play in the slice is one level: expand to the fields and found outposts there (G65), keep its economy running, research toward its library, remember what it has seen, hold a trader's radius, defend, and attack in waves once its fleet outweighs the enemy it remembers. Its memory starts from the other start, which the symmetric sector makes known (G66), and a wave needs a sighting of the enemy's fleet within the last few minutes, so it does not attack at tick zero (panel P6). G-M5's design sets the rest, from the first review's model (F5.2). A stronger opponent is a design of its own.

**Difficulty** (G67). The skirmish's options may handicap either side with a starting bank and an income multiplier, from half to double, shown to both sides at the start and at the end. That is the opponent's difficulty, as Warzone's power levels are, except that nothing is hidden. A handicap is a rule of the match for the side it is set for; it changes nothing the opponent sees or does, so R19 holds. The batch's scripted sides play through sessions as it does (G73), and the two-library runner fields it against any library, which after the slice becomes the library challenge (G69).

It is game code that reads messages and writes commands, so it belongs neither in `GameLogic`, which holds the world, nor in `GameLib`, which draws it. A project of its own, referencing only `NeuronCore` and the shared game library (§11.1), makes that boundary one the compiler keeps. It starts in G-M2, as the battle bench's scripted second side, and like every new project it is an ADR (`AGENTS.md` §2). What the compiler cannot keep, `AGENTS.md` R19 does (G27), and R21 holds it to the tick and the seed, as it holds the world (G53).

### 11.2 The load, by arithmetic

These figures are arithmetic on assumed counts, not measurements: each side at its command budget, taken as fifty ships a side, each a hull of one part and eight modules; fifteen structures a side, outposts included, each a hull and two modules; ships of 3,000 voxels and structures of 20,000 on average. The command budget (G56) is what holds the counts: its costs are set so that no fleet within it exceeds this section's placements and voxels.

- **Placements:** 2 × (50 × 9 + 15 × 3) = 990, where the default space scene has 52 (SpaceScene §5.2). Seen by the camera and the one shadow map the slice keeps (G22), that is up to 1,980 draws a frame, and 3,960 once S-M7's three cascades land, one `DrawIndexedInstanced` each as `SplatPass` issues them today.
- **Voxels:** 2 × (50 × 3,000 + 15 × 20,000) = 900,000, within the default space scene's 1,033,408. The classes' voxel budgets (G40) are what hold a fleet to such counts: in the capital ship's box alone, a hundred solid bricks would hold 7.3 million voxels.
- **Snapshots:** 130 ships and structures at today's 48 bytes each are 6,240 bytes a tick for a side that sees them all, and 187,200 bytes a second at 30 ticks. Over a loopback that is nothing; over a network it wants the delta encoding the UDP design adds.
- **Damage events:** at an assumed battle peak of fifty lasers each removing one or two voxels a tick and fifty shells a second each removing about fifty, some 4,850 voxels go each second: 10 to 15 KB a second at two or three bytes a voxel, for a side that sees it all. The snapshots, not the damage, are what a network needs delta encoding for.
- **Designs** cross once each: 42,988 bytes for one the size of the capital ship, at R14's four bytes a voxel.
- **The match's worst frame** comes late: the live ships, structures and fields, the wrecks and debris their lifetimes keep (G50), and each second's events. The battle's bench reports it beside its median (gate 4).

So the load moves from voxels to placements and events. The renderer was built for the first; the second is what G-M2's bench must measure, and gate 1 measures whole placements before G-M1.

**The batch** (G32, G73). A headless batch of the opponent against itself and against scripted sides, seeded, in lockstep, each seed played from both starts (G66), runs from G-M2 on and writes the telemetry that the gates and §14's risks read: the opponent's decisions, with their inputs; the server's economy, minute by minute; its engagements, with the designs, bearings, shots and hits in each, and the shots a side's own hulls stopped; every command refused; an after-action record for each design, which G-M5's end screen also shows the player (G69); and a summary of match length, winner and cause, wins by design pair and range band, and wins by start. **Risks:**

- **Sculpting inside a strategy game.** The library keeps the clock away from the sculpting (G5), which works only if a refit to a variant is enough to answer what happens in a match. Gate 10 asks whether the owner and the outside players refit in answer to what they see, and G-M5 and G-M6 show the rest.
- **The designer comes late** (G45). Its sculpting core arrives beside G-M4 and its rules last, so what sets the game apart is still among the last things built. The time box decides it: past G-M5's end, the slice ships with MagicaVoxel as its hull tool, which works, but is not the game's construction.
- **MagicaVoxel's conventions** (NVF §11). Until the designer's core, a design's mounts are MagicaVoxel markers (§5.2), named by `_name` and turned by `_r`, both undocumented conventions of its file. The reader's tests pin today's behavior, and the importer refuses what it does not recognize.
- **A mount refined in Blender can drift** (NVF §11). Its position is kept in its part's space, from the grid's minimum corner, so growing the part toward negative coordinates in MagicaVoxel moves the mount off its voxels, and nothing refuses it; a mount authored as a marker moves with its voxels. G37's validation catches the drift only where the mount's box then holds a hull voxel or no longer shares a face with one.
- **The rules that pick the winning hull.** Aim, leading, targets and bearing are chosen (G57, G58, G71, G62), and the sweep's contract, exposure and validation are written (G37, G38, G39). What stays a risk is what the chosen rules favor. Gate 5 fields shapes chosen to exploit them, a ring and a hollow shell among them, and runs again whenever prices or rules change (G73).
- **The brick and the needle.** §5.4's principles stay principles until tuned, and balance is iteration, not design. Gate 5, the shape test, is the test: after two failed retunes the damage table held in reserve comes in (G60), and after a third the owner reopens G18 before command is built.
- **Swarm or capital.** What size is worth at equal price is not fixed by the rules: on the panel's model it swings about forty times between a square law, where every ship fires, and a linear law, where only a front rank can (B2). Command points bound the count (G56), and gate 5's small ships against one large measure the rest.
- **Per-voxel damage at scale** is the largest technical cost (§8.4). If the battle's bench finds it too dear against G32's budgets, the fallbacks come in G44's order: lifetimes and levers, then a smaller command budget, and damage in coarser cells only last, and only with an equally coarse build grid. Module hit points are not one, since G6 rules them out.
- **Placements, not voxels** (§11.2): nineteen times the placements, and up to 1,980 draws a frame with the slice's one shadow map, or 3,960 with S-M7's cascades. Gate 1 measures whole placements before G-M1; `ExecuteIndirect` is the expected answer, and the bench decides.
- **Vertex work on small voxels** (SpaceScene §17). A view of a sector puts most of its voxels under a pixel, and the splat pays four vertex invocations for each whatever its size; on the owner's GPU, M5's note found pixel-shader lanes cost nothing measurable, so the cost is expected in the vertices. S-M9's coarse models are the first answer (question 36).
- **Debris is the heaviest workload** (SpaceScene §17). A detonated placement's voxels are all oriented, none can be culled as hidden or drawn coarse, and the splat evaluates each one's pose at four vertices in every view that draws it. Gate 4 measures the aftermath, and if the pose's time is what fails it, SpaceScene's lead is posing each voxel once a frame, in a compute pass that every splat reads.
- **Own hulls block fire** (G39). So formations, bands and the opponent must all keep lines of fire clear, and command is harder than in a game where shots pass friends. Squadrons fly by bearing (G62), a weapon holds fire on a blocked line and ships prefer clear ones (G71), and gate 6 sets a threshold on the shots a side's own hulls stop.
- **The fog is a property of the protocol** (G30), and of what each message holds: a shot carries no origin (G54). A message type added without its per-side rule leaks. The batch runs the opponent with and without its enemy's internals, which is the check.
- **The economy's numbers decide whether a match ends in its target.** The exchange rate, the ore budget, the command budget, the research minute and the cap on labs are G-M4's to set against its budget table, and the batch measures the length (G36, G42, G56, G64).
- **Salvage rewards the defender.** A failed attack pays the side it attacked, though never more than half the hull it lost (G63), which still favors defense. The clock ends a stalemate (G36), and gate 9's raid followed by a turtle measures the edge.
- **A reactor is a kill switch the player can aim for** (G51). "Attack this module" can pick one, since only the command module is off its list (§9). The readout leads with the weakest vital module and a retreat window is tuned in (G59); if that proves one click too close, reactors leave the list as well (G33).
- **Research reaches the field at once** (G55). A side ahead in research is ahead on every ship at once, as in Warzone. G64's minute and the trader's step beyond are the counterweights, and the batch reads the lead minute by minute.
- **Outposts can creep.** An outpost's station grows only within its build radius (G61), and a side holds two (G65); gate 9's exploit sides are where towers at the enemy's field would show.
- **Symmetric sectors trade variety for fairness** (G66). Half of each sector is the other half turned; seeds still vary the half.
- **An opponent is a project.** One that plays convincingly is a large piece of work; the slice's plays at one level, with the handicap as its difficulty (G67), and a weak opponent makes the skirmish a test bed rather than a game. Gate 8 holds it to beating a scripted rush and a scripted turtle, and gate 9 to standing up to the exploits.
- **The opponent's library is frozen.** A design that beats the whole of it beats it every match. The opponent fields any library, the batch pits the libraries against each other (§10), and the library challenge makes that a mode after the slice (G69).
- **Few human judges.** The owner and three to five outside players (G68) are a signal, not a statistic; the batch is where the numbers come from.
- **The UI is the tree's own.** D9 rules out third-party code, so the HUD, the refit screen and the designer are built on the canvas, from the panels up, and §9's schedule is the toolkit's specification. A toolkit would need the owner to revise D9.
- **Determinism holds within one build** (SpaceScene §17). That is enough for a skirmish in one process, and G41's stepping and command log make a skirmish replay, while R21 keeps the world and the opponent off the wall clock. Multiplayer across x64 and ARM64 stays authoritative, as the engine already is, rather than lockstep.
- **The server's sleep** (SpaceScene §17). On Windows `sleep_until` can wake milliseconds late; ticks are stamped exactly, and the interpolation delay absorbs it. A high-resolution timer would need Windows headers in `NeuronServer`, which ADR-015 forecloses until the bench shows `sleep_until` waking too late; but the bench, like the batch, steps the server in lockstep, so only a skirmish on the server's thread can show it.
- **The phase is long:** six milestones before a skirmish is whole. The slice keeps its content small, about twenty research items, a dozen modules and two weapons, so that the whole loop fits.
